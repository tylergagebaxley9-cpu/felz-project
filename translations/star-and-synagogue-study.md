# The Star of Your God / The Synagogue of Satan

A study of four passages: Amos 5:26, Acts 7:43, Revelation 2:9, Revelation 3:9

All English quotations are KJV (public domain).

---

## 1. Amos 5:26

> "But ye have borne the tabernacle of your Moloch and Chiun your images, the star of your god, which ye made to yourselves."

**Date and setting.** Amos prophesied in the northern kingdom of Israel around 760–750 BC, during the reign of Jeroboam II — a period of military success and economic prosperity. The book's core charge is that prosperity coexisted with judicial corruption, exploitation of the poor, and syncretistic worship. Chapter 5 is a funeral lament over Israel; verses 21–27 are God's rejection of Israel's festivals and offerings, ending in the sentence of exile.

**The key Hebrew terms.**

| Hebrew (consonants) | KJV | What it likely is |
|---|---|---|
| *skkwt* (סכות) | "tabernacle" | Probably *Sakkut*, an Akkadian astral deity name (Sakkut/SAG.KUD), associated with Saturn. KJV read it as *sukkat*, "booth/tabernacle." |
| *kywn* (כיון) | "Chiun" | *Kaiwan*, the Akkadian *kayamānu*, "the steady one" — the standard Mesopotamian name for the planet Saturn. |

The Masoretic vowel points on both words appear to be borrowed from *shiqquts* (שִׁקּוּץ), "detestable thing" — a scribal device also used for names like Molech. So the pointing is a theological comment, not a phonetic record.

**"The star of your god."** This is the whole description. No shape, no number of points, no color, no geometry. The phrase functions as an apposition identifying the images just named: these idols *are* the astral emblem of a planetary deity. In the ancient Near East, Saturn and other planetary gods were typically depicted by an eight-pointed rosette or star, a winged disk, or a simple radiate mark — conventions well attested in Assyrian and Babylonian boundary stones and seals.

**The sentence.** Amos 5:27: "Therefore will I cause you to go into captivity beyond Damascus." Damascus marks the direction of the Assyrian deportation that came in 722 BC.

## 2. Acts 7:43

> "Yea, ye took up the tabernacle of Moloch, and the star of your god Remphan, figures which ye made to worship them: and I will carry you away beyond Babylon."

**Setting.** Stephen's speech before the Sanhedrin, c. AD 34. It is a rapid survey of Israel's history structured around a single thesis: God's presence was never confined to a building, and the people repeatedly rejected the deliverers God sent. Verses 39–43 cover the golden calf and its consequences. Stephen is executed immediately after finishing.

**Two differences from Amos.**

1. **Remphan / Rephan** (Greek Ῥαιφάν, with manuscript variants Rompha, Rephan, Romphan). This comes from the Septuagint of Amos 5:26, not from the Hebrew. The most common explanation is a transcription slip somewhere in the Greek tradition: Hebrew *kaph* (כ) misread as *resh* (ר), yielding *Rywn* → *Raiphan*. An older suggestion connects it to a Coptic/Egyptian name for Saturn. Either way, Rephan and Kaiwan are the same deity behind two spellings.

2. **"Beyond Babylon"** instead of Amos's "beyond Damascus." Stephen (or his source) updates the geography to the later and more famous Babylonian exile, which better fits an address to a Jerusalem audience.

**Why it matters.** Stephen is quoting a text of judgment against Israel's ancestors and applying it to his hearers. The rhetorical target is idolatry and resistance to God's messengers. This is prophetic self-criticism from inside the tradition — the same genre as Amos.

## 3. Revelation 2:9 — to Smyrna

> "I know thy works, and tribulation, and poverty, (but thou art rich) and I know the blasphemy of them which say they are Jews, and are not, but are the synagogue of Satan."

## 4. Revelation 3:9 — to Philadelphia

> "Behold, I will make them of the synagogue of Satan, which say they are Jews, and are not, but do lie; behold, I will make them to come and worship before thy feet, and to know that I have loved thee."

**Setting.** Two of the seven letters to churches in the Roman province of Asia (western Turkey), most commonly dated to the mid-90s AD under Domitian, though some date the book to the late 60s. Smyrna and Philadelphia are the only two of the seven churches that receive no rebuke.

**Notes.**

- "Satan" here is the Greek *Satanas*, from Hebrew *satan*, "adversary/accuser." The phrase is best read as "assembly of the adversary" — a counter-claim about who is really opposing God.
- *Synagōgē* simply means "assembly" or "gathering." James 2:2 uses it of a Christian meeting. It is not a technical slur in itself; the force is in the genitive that follows.
- The stated grievance is a dispute over the name "Jew" — a claim about who legitimately belongs to Israel. In Philadelphia, the promise is that the opponents will eventually acknowledge the church, which presumes a future reconciliation, not annihilation.

**Interpretive options.** These are genuinely debated; the main positions:

1. **Intra-Jewish sectarian polemic.** The dominant scholarly reading. The author is a Jewish follower of Jesus writing to communities in conflict with local synagogue authorities, probably over exclusion, denunciation to Roman officials, or access to the legal exemptions Jews held regarding the imperial cult. The rhetoric belongs to the same family as the Qumran sect calling other Jews "the congregation of Belial," or the prophets calling Jerusalem "Sodom" (Isaiah 1:10). Insiders insulting other insiders.
2. **Gentile pretenders.** A minority view: the "false Jews" are non-Jews claiming Jewish identity for the legal protections it carried.
3. **A general theological category.** Later Christian readings extend the phrase into a timeless label. This is the reading with the worst downstream history — it was central to centuries of Christian anti-Jewish polemic, and Revelation 2:9 and 3:9 remain among the most quoted verses in antisemitic literature. Worth knowing regardless of where you land, because it shapes how the verses land on a modern reader.

## 5. On linking the "star" to the hexagram

You raised the possibility that the star in Amos and Acts is the six-pointed star. Setting out the evidence plainly:

**What supports a connection:** Both texts do condemn an astral emblem tied to Saturn worship. Saturn is genuinely the planet behind Kaiwan/Rephan. And the six-pointed star did later become the principal emblem of Jewish identity. If one assumes symbolic continuity, the chain looks suggestive.

**What weighs against it:** The chain doesn't hold historically.

- Amos gives no shape at all. The identification requires supplying a detail the text withholds.
- The standard astral iconography in 8th-century BC Mesopotamia and the Levant was the **eight-pointed** star or rosette for Ishtar/Venus, with winged disks and crescents for other deities. A six-pointed hexagram is not the expected emblem for Saturn in that period.
- The hexagram appears in antiquity as a widespread decorative and apotropaic motif across many cultures — Hindu, Islamic, Christian, Jewish — with no fixed meaning. It is found on a stone in the Capernaum synagogue (3rd–4th c. AD) alongside a pentagram and a swastika-like motif, i.e., as ornament.
- Its use as a specifically *Jewish* identifier is medieval and later: Prague's Jewish community was granted a flag bearing it in the 14th century; it spread widely from there, and became a national symbol only in the 19th–20th centuries via the Zionist movement. That's roughly 2,000 years after Amos and 1,300 after Stephen.
- The Amos→hexagram identification appears to originate in 20th-century polemical literature rather than in the exegetical tradition. It is worth knowing that the argument's modern home is largely in Christian Identity and related antisemitic material, where its function is to assert that contemporary Jews worship Saturn/Satan. That provenance doesn't automatically make a claim false, but it does explain why the argument keeps circulating despite the chronology not working — and it's a reason to hold it loosely.

**Bottom line:** the strong claim ("Amos condemned the hexagram") is not sustainable on the evidence. The weaker claim ("Amos condemned Saturn worship") is well supported and doesn't require the hexagram at all.

## 7. Study questions

1. Why does Stephen change "beyond Damascus" to "beyond Babylon"? What does the substitution do for his argument?
2. Amos 5:21–24 rejects worship and demands justice; 5:25–27 charges idolatry. Are these one indictment or two?
3. In Revelation 3:9, the opponents come and worship at the church's feet. What does that outcome imply about what the author thinks of them?
4. Both Amos and Stephen aim their harshest words at their own people. What changes when a later reader aims the same words at someone else's?
