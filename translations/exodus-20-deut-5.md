# Exodus 20 · Deuteronomy 5 — the Ten Words

Translated from the Hebrew. No English translation was consulted or adapted.
Method: `translations/OUR-TRANSLATION.md`.

Two things make this passage different from everything else in this book.

**First**, the Bible gives the Ten Commandments **twice**, and the two versions do
not match. Not a manuscript variant — both are in every Hebrew Bible, forty-odd
chapters apart, and they differ in wording, in order, and in the reason given for
the Sabbath.

**Second**, the Samaritan Pentateuch carries **an entire additional commandment**
that appears in no Jewish or Christian Bible. Not a phrase. A paragraph.

---

# I. The Samaritan tenth commandment

## The size of it, counted

| Witness | Exodus 20:17 |
|---|---:|
| Masoretic (Leningrad, 1008 CE) | **18 words** |
| **Samaritan Pentateuch** | **148 words** |

The Samaritan text runs the Masoretic verse — the prohibition on coveting — and
then continues for another 130 words.

## What the extra material says

Translated from the Samaritan Hebrew:

> **And it shall be, when YHWH your God brings you to the land of the Canaanite,
> where you are going in to possess it, you shall set up for yourself great stones
> and coat them with plaster, and you shall write on the stones all the words of
> this instruction. And it shall be, when you cross the Jordan, you shall set up
> these stones which I am commanding you today, on Mount Gerizim.**

## Key words

**וְהֵקֵמֹתָ** — hiphil of **קום** (*to rise · to stand*). Causative: **"you shall set up"** — the ordinary verb for erecting stones or a pillar.

**וְשַׂדְתָּ … בַּשִּׂיד** — root **שׂיד**, *lime · plaster*. The verb and its object share the root: **"you shall coat them with plaster"**.

**כָּל־דִּבְרֵי הַתּוֹרָה הַזֹּאת** — **תּוֹרָה** is from **ירה**, *to throw · to shoot · to point out · to instruct*. Its base sense is **instruction · direction · teaching**, not "law" in the legal sense that English imports. **"all the words of this instruction"**

**בְּהַרְגְּרִזִים** — **on Mount Gerizim**. In Samaritan texts this is written as one word.

## Where this is not invented

The material is not fabricated from nothing. Nearly all of it appears in the Jewish
and Christian Bible too — at **Deuteronomy 27:2–8** — where Israel is told to set up
plastered stones and write the instruction on them when they cross the Jordan.

**The difference is location, and it is one letter's worth of consequence.** In the
Masoretic Deuteronomy 27:4 the stones go up on **Mount Ebal**. In the Samaritan
they go up on **Mount Gerizim**.

That single word is the hinge of the whole Samaritan–Jewish division: which
mountain is the legitimate place of worship. It is the same dispute the Samaritan
woman raises at John 4:20.

## What can and cannot be concluded

**Established:** the Samaritan Pentateuch contains this commandment; the Masoretic
does not; the material largely duplicates Deuteronomy 27; the mountains differ.

**Not established — and this is where honesty costs something:** which came first.
The Samaritan text can be read as an expansion inserted to ground Gerizim's claim
in the Decalogue itself. The Masoretic can be read as having relocated the site to
Ebal. **Each side's account requires the other side's text to be the altered one,
and the manuscripts do not adjudicate that.**

One piece of evidence is worth reporting precisely because it complicates the
usual assumption: a fragment of Deuteronomy among the Dead Sea Scrolls, and the
Old Latin, have been argued to support **Gerizim** at Deut 27:4. That evidence is
contested and I have not verified it from this corpus — it is flagged as an open
question, not offered as a finding.

# II. The two Decalogues disagree with each other

Both versions are in every Hebrew Bible. Neither is a variant of the other in any
manuscript sense. They simply differ.

## The Sabbath — two different reasons

**Exodus 20:11** grounds the Sabbath in **creation**:

> **For in six days YHWH made the heavens and the earth, the sea and all that is in
> them, and he rested on the seventh day. Therefore YHWH blessed the day of the
> Sabbath and set it apart.**

**Deuteronomy 5:15** grounds it in **the Exodus**:

> **And you shall remember that you were a slave in the land of Egypt, and YHWH
> your God brought you out from there with a strong hand and an outstretched arm.
> Therefore YHWH your God commanded you to keep the day of the Sabbath.**

Same commandment. Two entirely different justifications — cosmological in one,
historical in the other. Both begin `עַל־כֵּן`, "therefore", and reach the same
obligation from opposite premises.

**וַיָּנַח** (Exod 20:11) — root **נוח**, *to rest · to settle · to come to rest*. It is not the same root as **שָׁבַת** (*to cease · to desist*), from which "Sabbath" comes. The Hebrew distinguishes ceasing from resting; English usually does not.

## The tenth — different order, different verb, different list

| | Exodus 20:17 | Deuteronomy 5:21 |
|---|---|---|
| first object | **בֵּית** — house | **אֵשֶׁת** — wife |
| second object | **אֵשֶׁת** — wife | **בֵּית** — house |
| second verb | **תַחְמֹד** — same verb repeated | **תִתְאַוֶּה** — a *different* verb |
| includes field? | **no** | **שָׂדֵהוּ** — yes |

**Three differences, not one.**

**חמד** — *to desire · to take pleasure in · to covet*. The word is not inherently negative; it is used of things that are desirable, including in a good sense.

**אוה** (hitpael **תִתְאַוֶּה**) — *to crave · to long for · to have an appetite for*. Closer to appetite than to acquisitive desire.

Deuteronomy uses **two different verbs** where Exodus repeats one. And Deuteronomy
puts the neighbour's **wife** first, separated from the property list; Exodus puts
the **house** first, with the wife inside the list of holdings. That ordering is
not a trivial matter of style — it changes what the commandment treats the wife as.

**Also worth noting:** neither list is what most people recite. Both include the
slave, the female slave, the ox and the donkey.

# III. What this passage establishes

1. **The Ten Commandments exist in two non-identical versions inside the canon
   itself.** This requires no manuscript hunting — open any Bible to both places.
2. **A third tradition carries an additional commandment**, and it is a substantial
   paragraph rather than a phrase.
3. **The differences are theologically weighted.** Creation versus Exodus as the
   ground of the Sabbath. Gerizim versus Ebal as the place of worship. The wife's
   position relative to property.
4. **Direction of change is not establishable from the manuscripts** in either the
   Samaritan case or the Exodus/Deuteronomy case. What can be shown is that the
   traditions differ, where, and by how much.

The usual framing — that the Samaritans "added" a commandment — is one available
account. It is not the only one the evidence permits, and this book does not assert
it.
