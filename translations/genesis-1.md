## Genesis 1:1

### Word by word

**בְּרֵאשִׁית** · consonants `בראשית` · parse `HR/Ncfsa` — preposition בְּ + noun, common feminine singular **absolute**
> Root **ראש** (head, top). רֵאשִׁית is the abstract noun built on it with the
> -ִית suffix (the same pattern as אַחֲרִית "end/afterward" and תַּכְלִית
> "completion/limit"). Range: *beginning · first part · first/choicest portion
> (as in "firstfruits") · start of a span of time.*
>
> **This is the passage's first real grammatical fork, and it does not resolve
> cleanly. Both readings below are live.**
>
> **Reading A — absolute, "In the beginning":** רֵאשִׁית stands alone, governing
> nothing, functioning adverbially: "at the beginning / at first." Evidence:
> (1) the noun is elsewhere used exactly this way with no following genitive —
> Isaiah 46:10 has מֵרֵאשִׁית ("from the beginning") standing free, not bound to
> anything after it; (2) the traditional Masoretic cantillation places a
> disjunctive accent (tifcha) under רֵאשִׁית, marking a syntactic break before
> what follows — the Masoretic tradition itself read it as not tightly bound
> forward. On this reading, v.1 is a complete independent sentence, and v.2
> describes the state of things at that point.
>
> **Reading B — construct, "When God began to...":** רֵאשִׁית is in construct
> with the finite clause that follows (בָּרָא אֱלֹהִים אֵת... as a whole
> functioning as its "genitive"). Evidence: (1) a noun in construct governing
> a finite verb clause (rather than an infinitive) is attested elsewhere in
> Biblical Hebrew — Hosea 1:2 has תְּחִלַּת דִּבֶּר־יְהוָה, "the start of, YHWH
> spoke," the identical syntactic pattern with a different construct noun; (2)
> more importantly, **the construct and absolute forms of רֵאשִׁית are
> spelled identically** — nouns of this -ִית pattern do not change shape
> between states (compare אַחֲרִית, likewise invariant). That means the `Ncfsa`
> "absolute" tag above is not a form-based fact; it is the tagger's judgment
> call, following the Masoretic tradition's own reading — the bare letters
> cannot settle this by themselves. On this reading, v.1 is a subordinate
> temporal clause and the main verb of the sentence doesn't arrive until v.3
> ("...and God said, let there be light").
>
> **What decides between them is not in the word itself.** רֵאשִׁית permits
> both; the deciding evidence (the accent, the absence of the article, the
> Isaiah parallel, the Hosea parallel) points in different directions and
> none of it is airtight. This translation renders it as Reading A below
> because that is the more self-contained rendering in English, but Reading B
> is grammatically live and is given in full underneath.

**בָּרָא** · `ברא` · `HVqp3ms` — verb, qal perfect, 3rd masculine singular
> Root **ברא**. In the qal stem this verb's subject is, without exception in
> the Hebrew Bible, God — it is never used of a human agent making something,
> unlike עשה ("make," used of both God and people) or יצר ("form, shape," used
> of a potter). Range: *to create · to bring into existence.* Perfect aspect:
> a completed act. **"created"**

**אֱלֹהִים** · `אלהים` · `HNcmpa` — noun, common masculine **plural** absolute
> Root **אלה/אלוה**. The -ִים ending is the ordinary Hebrew masculine plural
> marker, and this exact spelling is used elsewhere in the Bible with plural
> reference and plural verb agreement ("other gods," "the gods of the
> nations"). Here, though, every verb bound to it in this passage is singular
> — בָּרָא (3ms), and later יֹּאמֶר, יַּרְא, יַּבְדֵּל, יִּקְרָא, all 3ms. That
> singular agreement is the plain grammatical marker that a plural-form noun
> is being used with a singular referent — the standard account is a "plural
> of intensification," a category with parallels among other Hebrew abstract
> nouns.
>
> **What this does and does not establish:** the grammar plainly shows the
> word is being used as a singular here — that part is not in doubt. What it
> does *not* establish is any claim about the internal nature of the referent;
> singular agreement with a plural-form noun is a routine feature of the
> language (the same word can and elsewhere does take plural agreement when a
> plural referent is meant), and grammar alone cannot adjudicate a theological
> reading either way. Range: *God · a god · gods · divine beings.* **"God"**

**אֵת** · `את` · `HTo` — particle, direct-object marker
> Not a word with independent meaning — a grammatical particle marking the
> noun that follows as the direct object of the verb. It is not translated
> into English; English marks the object by word order instead. (Distinct
> from the preposition אֵת "with," which is spelled and pointed the same but
> is a different word.)

**הַשָּׁמַיִם** · `השמים` · `HTd/Ncmpa` — article + noun, common masculine plural absolute
> Root uncertain — no clearly attested singular form is in ordinary use, and
> proposed etymologies (e.g. tying it to מַיִם "water") are disputed rather
> than established; the range is stated from usage, not from etymology.
> Grammatically it behaves like מַיִם and פָּנִים — a noun that is plural in
> form but names one thing. Range: *sky · heavens (the visible vault above) ·
> the region above the earth.* **"the heavens" / "the sky"**

**וְאֵת הָאָרֶץ** · `וארץ` (with `את` again) · `HC/To` + `HTd/Ncbsa`
> **אֵת** as above. **אָרֶץ**, root **ארץ**: range *earth (the planet or its
> surface) · land (a specific territory) · ground (soil).* Here the cosmic
> sense, paired with "the heavens." **"and the earth"**

### The verse — Reading A

> **In the beginning God created the heavens and the earth.**

### The verse — Reading B

> **When God began to create the heavens and the earth —**
> *(the sentence continues into v.2, and the main clause doesn't land until v.3.)*

## Genesis 1:2

### Word by word

**וְהָאָרֶץ** · `וארץ` · `HC/Td/Ncbsa` — conjunction + article + noun, as above
> Now the grammatical subject of the clause. **"And the earth" / "Now the earth"**

**הָיְתָה** · `היתה` · `HVqp3fs` — verb, qal perfect, 3rd feminine singular
> Root **היה**: *to be · to exist · to become · to happen.* Grammatical note:
> Hebrew has a distinct construction for "became [something]" — הָיָה + לְ. Here
> there is no לְ; the verb takes the noun תֹהוּ directly as a predicate, which
> is the ordinary Biblical Hebrew pattern for a simple stative "was," not a
> change-of-state "became." The "became" reading (sometimes argued on
> theological grounds, that the earth *turned* formless) is available in
> principle since הָיָה can carry that sense elsewhere, but it is the weaker
> reading here — this specific construction, without לְ, is not the one the
> language normally uses to signal transformation. **"was"**

**תֹהוּ** · `תהו` · `HNcmsa` — noun, common masculine singular absolute
> Root **תהה/תהו**. Used elsewhere of a trackless desert waste, and
> metaphorically of nothingness or futility (idols called תֹהוּ, "empty
> things"). Range: *formlessness · waste · emptiness · void · that which has
> no substance.* **"formless" / "void"**

**וָבֹהוּ** · `ובהו` · `HC/Ncmsa` — conjunction + noun, common masculine singular absolute
> Root **בהה/בהו** — genuinely uncertain. This word occurs three times in the
> whole Hebrew Bible, and *every* occurrence is in the fixed pair תֹהוּ וָבֹהוּ.
> It never stands alone. Its independent semantic range cannot honestly be
> established from usage, because there is no independent usage to draw on —
> stating a confident range here would be padding. What can be said: as part
> of a rhyming, alliterative pair, it functions to intensify and echo תֹהוּ
> rather than to introduce a distinct new idea — a common feature of Hebrew
> word-pairs of this shape. **"and void"** (as an echo of "formless," not a
> separately defined second concept)

**וְחֹשֶׁךְ** · `וחשך` · `HC/Ncmsa` — conjunction + noun, common masculine singular absolute
> Root **חשך**. Range: *darkness (absence of light) · obscurity · gloom,
> calamity (metaphorical extension elsewhere).* Here, literal. **"and darkness"**

**עַל־פְּנֵי** · `על` + `פני` · `HR` + `HNcbpc` — preposition + noun, common both plural **construct**
> פְּנֵי is the construct plural of פָּנִים, "face." עַל־פְּנֵי is the standing
> idiom "upon the face of" = "on/over the surface of." **"upon the surface of"**

**תְהוֹם** · `תהום` · `HNcbsa` — noun, common both singular absolute
> Root **תהם**. Not simply "a deep place" — in the wider Semitic context this
> is the primeval, cosmic body of water. Range: *the deep · the abyss · the
> primeval ocean.* **"the deep"**

**וְרוּחַ אֱלֹהִים** · `ורוח` + `אלהים` · `HC/Ncbsc` + `HNcmpa` — conjunction + noun (construct) + noun
> Root **רוח**. This is the second real fork in the passage. Its range
> genuinely covers: *wind (moving air) · breath (of a living creature) ·
> spirit (an animating or emotional force) · disposition, temperament.* All
> four senses are attested across the root's occurrences; none is the
> "primary" one from which the others derive — this is a case where the range
> should not be collapsed to a single English word.
>
> With אֱלֹהִים construct after it, two readings are both grammatically live:
> **"a wind from God" / "a mighty wind"** — Hebrew idiom elsewhere uses "of
> God" as an intensifier/superlative on a following noun (a comparable
> pattern appears with other nouns taking אֱלֹהִים or אֵל as a superlative
> modifier); on this reading רוּחַ אֱלֹהִים names a very great or divinely-sent
> windstorm, a natural-world referent. **"the spirit/breath of God"** — the
> ordinary construct sense, naming a divine entity or presence in its own
> right, in motion over the waters. Nothing in the grammar of the phrase
> itself picks between these; both are attested uses of the same
> construction elsewhere in the language.

**מְרַחֶפֶת** · `מרחפת` · `HVprfsa` — verb, piel participle, feminine singular
> Root **רחף** — rare. It occurs only here and at Deuteronomy 32:11, of an
> eagle over its young. Because the range must be drawn from just two
> occurrences, it should be stated narrowly rather than padded: *a hovering
> or fluttering motion, especially of wings in the air.* The Deut 32:11
> parallel (an eagle stirring up its nest, hovering over its young) attaches
> a protective, caring nuance to the physical motion in that occurrence — that
> connotation is textually grounded in the parallel, not invented, but it
> rests on a single comparison point. Participle: an ongoing action in
> progress. **"hovering"**

**עַל־פְּנֵי הַמָּיִם** · as above · **"upon the surface of the waters"**

### The verse — both readings of רוּחַ אֱלֹהִים

> **Reading 1:** *Now the earth was formless and void, and darkness was upon
> the surface of the deep, and a mighty wind was sweeping over the surface of
> the waters.*
>
> **Reading 2:** *Now the earth was formless and void, and darkness was upon
> the surface of the deep, and the spirit of God was hovering over the
> surface of the waters.*

## Genesis 1:3

### Word by word

**וַיֹּאמֶר** · `ויאמר` · `HC/Vqw3ms` — conjunction + verb, qal wayyiqtol, 3ms
> Root **אמר**: *to say · to speak · to command* (the "command" sense surfaces
> when, as here, direct speech follows in a volitional form). Wayyiqtol is
> the ordinary Hebrew narrative past tense — "and X said," moving the story
> forward. **"And God said"**

**אֱלֹהִים** · as above.

**יְהִי** · `יהי` · `HVqj3ms` — verb, qal **jussive**, 3rd masculine singular
> Root **היה**, as above. The jussive is a distinct volitional form — a
> command or wish ("let it be / may it be"), grammatically different from a
> plain future ("it will be"). This is a precise grammatical fact, not a
> stylistic choice: the form itself marks this as a command. **"Let there be"**

**אוֹר** · `אור` · `HNcbsa` — noun, common both singular absolute
> Root **אור**: range *light (physical illumination) · daylight ·
> (metaphorically, elsewhere) well-being, favor.* Here, physical light.
> **"light"**

**וַיְהִי** · `ויהי` · `HC/Vqw3ms` — conjunction + verb, qal wayyiqtol, 3ms
> Same root, now the ordinary narrative form rather than the jussive — the
> shift from יְהִי (command) to וַיְהִי (narrated event) marks the shift from
> the command to its accomplishment, in the same verb. **"and there was"**

**אוֹר** · as above. **"light"**

### The verse

> **And God said, "Let there be light" — and there was light.**

## Genesis 1:4

### Word by word

**וַיַּרְא** · `וירא` · `HC/Vqw3ms` — conjunction + verb, qal wayyiqtol, 3ms
> Root **ראה**: *to see · to perceive · to observe · to examine, evaluate.*
> The evaluative sense ("see that...") is active here, governing the כִּי
> clause that follows. **"And God saw"**

**אֶת־הָאוֹר** · object marker + "the light," as above.

**כִּי־טוֹב** · `כי` + `טוב` · `HC` + `HAamsa` — conjunction + adjective
> כִּי here introduces the content of what was seen ("that..."). **טוֹב**,
> root **טוב**: range *good · pleasing · functional, fit for its purpose ·
> beneficial.* **"that it was good"**

**וַיַּבְדֵּל** · `ויבדל` · `HC/Vhw3ms` — conjunction + verb, hiphil wayyiqtol, 3ms
> Root **בדל**: qal is to be separate; hiphil (as here) is causative — to
> cause separation, divide, set apart. Range: *to separate · to divide · to
> distinguish.* **"and God divided"**

**בֵּין ... וּבֵין** · `בין` ×2 · `HR` + `HC/R`
> The standing double-preposition idiom for marking a boundary between two
> things: "between X and between Y" = "between X and Y." **"between... and"**

**הָאוֹר / הַחֹשֶׁךְ** · "the light" / "the darkness," as above.

### The verse

> **And God saw the light, that it was good, and God divided between the
> light and the darkness.**

## Genesis 1:5

### Word by word

**וַיִּקְרָא** · `ויקרא` · `HC/Vqw3ms` — conjunction + verb, qal wayyiqtol, 3ms
> Root **קרא**: range *to call · to call out, proclaim · to name · to
> summon · to read aloud.* With לְ on the object, as here, the idiom is "to
> call X [by the name] Y" = to name. **"And God called"**

**לָאוֹר / וְלַחֹשֶׁךְ** · preposition לְ + "the light" / "the darkness"

**יוֹם** · `יום` · `HNcmsa` — noun, common masculine singular absolute
> Root **יום**: range *day (a 24-hour span, or the daylight portion of one) ·
> daytime, as opposed to night · a period/time generally (idiomatic elsewhere,
> "in the day of X" = "at the time of X").* **"day"**

**קָרָא / לָיְלָה** · "he called" / **"night"** — root **ליל/לילה**, *night, nighttime.*

**וַיְהִי־עֶרֶב** · `ויהי` + `ערב` · verb as above + noun
> **עֶרֶב**, root **ערב**: *evening, dusk.* A proposed link to a separate root
> ערב meaning "to mix" (dusk as the mixing of light and dark) is sometimes
> made but is an uncertain etymology, not a settled derivation — noted here,
> not relied on. **"And there was evening"**

**וַיְהִי־בֹקֶר** · `ויהי` + `בקר`
> **בֹּקֶר**, root **בקר**: *morning, daybreak.* A proposed link to the
> separate root בקר "to inspect, seek out" (morning as the time one goes out
> to look over the flock) is likewise a suggested, not settled, connection.
> **"and there was morning"**

**יוֹם אֶחָד** · `יום` + `אחד` · `HNcmsa` + `HAcmsa` — noun + adjective/numeral
> **אֶחָד** is the **cardinal** numeral "one," not the ordinal "first"
> (רִאשׁוֹן). This is a plain grammatical fact worth flagging: starting with
> the second day, the text switches to ordinals (a second day, a third day...).
> Day one alone is counted, not ordered. **"day one"**

**On Hebrew day-reckoning:** the text states evening before morning —
עֶרֶב then בֹקֶר — in fixing what completes "one day." That order, evening
first, is in the word sequence itself, not an imported assumption; it is the
opposite of the morning-to-night frame an English reader brings by default.

### The verse

> **And God called the light Day, and the darkness he called Night. And there
> was evening, and there was morning — day one.**

## Witnesses

Checked against `hebrew-ot/morphhb` (Masoretic, Leningrad Codex, 1008 CE),
`dead-sea-scrolls/etcbc-dss` (three Qumran manuscripts overlap this passage),
and `septuagint/LXX-Rahlfs-1935`. Regenerate with `./tools/allwitness.py Gen 1 1`
through `5`; the manuscript-level detail below (which specific Qumran scroll
reads what) required tracing the corpus's `oslots`/`scroll` feature tables
directly, since `allwitness.py` reports the DSS reading as a single merged
line.

### Three Dead Sea Scrolls manuscripts, not one

Three separate Qumran manuscripts carry parts of Genesis 1:1–5:

- **4Q2** (4QGenᵇ) — all five verses
- **4Q7** (4QGenʰ¹) — all five verses
- **4Q8d** (4QGenʲ) — v.1 only, and only the opening two words

**4Q2 and 4Q7 agree with the Masoretic consonantal text on verses 1–4
exactly**, letter for letter.

### A real variant: verse 5, "day" vs. "by day"

At v.5, **4Q2 reads `יום`** (yom, "day") — matching the Masoretic text.
**4Q7 reads `יומם`** (yomam) instead, an adverbial form of the same root.

> 4Q2 / Masoretic: *"...and God called the light Day..."*
> 4Q7: *"...and God called the light 'by day' / 'in the daytime'..."*

This is a genuine three-consonant textual difference (`יום` vs `יומם`)
between two manuscripts of the same book, both centuries older than the
Leningrad Codex. It is a small variant with no doctrinal weight, but it is a
real one, and per this project's rule it is given here rather than silently
folded into the majority reading.

### An orthographic variant: verse 1, defective spelling

**4Q8d spells the first word `רשית`** — without the א — where 4Q2, 4Q7, and
the Masoretic text all have `ראשית`. This is a defective (unvocalized-with-א)
spelling of the identical word, not a different word or a different meaning;
it is included for completeness, not treated as a translated variant, because
it carries no semantic difference.

### The Septuagint at verse 2: translation, not manuscript variant

The LXX renders תֹהוּ וָבֹהוּ as `ἀόρατος καὶ ἀκατασκεύαστος` — "unseen /
invisible and unready / unformed" — and וְרוּחַ אֱלֹהִים מְרַחֶפֶת as
`πνεῦμα θεοῦ ἐπεφέρετο`, "a/the spirit of God was being borne along
(carried)." Both Qumran manuscripts agree with the Masoretic Hebrew here, so
there is no evidence of a different Hebrew source text behind the Greek at
this point — per `PROVENANCE.md`'s own guidance on reading disagreements,
this is the Greek translator's interpretive choice of vocabulary, not a
text-critical variant. It is worth naming because it shows a translator
resolving exactly the ambiguity flagged above (רוּחַ) toward "spirit," using
ἐπεφέρετο ("was being borne/carried") rather than a hovering-motion verb —
one ancient translator's answer to a question the Hebrew itself leaves open.

**LXX rendering of the whole passage, translated on its own terms (Greek,
not a report on the Hebrew):**

> *In the beginning God made the heaven and the earth. And the earth was
> unseen and unformed, and darkness was over the abyss, and the spirit of God
> was being carried over the water. And God said, "Let light come to be,"
> and light came to be. And God saw the light, that it was good, and God
> separated between the light and between the darkness. And God called the
> light Day, and the darkness he called Night, and evening came to be, and
> morning came to be, day one.*

The Greek confirms one detail independently: `ἡμέρα μία` uses the cardinal
"one," not the ordinal "first" (πρώτη) — agreeing with the Hebrew's אֶחָד
against the ordinal pattern used for the later days.
